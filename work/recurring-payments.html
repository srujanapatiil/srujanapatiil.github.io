Recurring payment orchestration

A shared scheduling system for recurring payments: daily, weekly, monthly and seasonal schedules with pauses, cancellations, payment-method changes and automated retries, built on AWS.

Organisation
The Shamaazi Project Ltd, UK fintech
Period
2023 – present
My role
Architect and engineer of the scheduling and retry services

01Business context and problem

Customers set up recurring payments that run on their own schedule: every day, every week, once a month, or every night through a fixed season. Several subscription products needed this, and each schedule had to keep working as customers paused, cancelled or changed their card.

The goal was one shared scheduling system that every product could rely on, rather than each product handling its own schedule logic.

What existed before the shared system (separate per-product logic, a legacy service?), and what problems that caused.

02Engineering constraints and technical challenge

  • Money moves. A duplicate charge is a customer-facing incident, so every step must be safe to repeat.
  • Load arrives in peaks. The busiest nights reach 50K+ payments, concentrated into a short window.
  • Schedules change mid-cycle. Pauses, cancellations and payment-method changes can happen at any time, including during a run.
  • Several products, one system. Frequencies and rules differ by product, but the engine must stay shared.
  • Two stacks live at once. Services were moving to TypeScript during this period, so contracts had to stay stable.

04Individual contribution and collaboration

What I did

  • Architected the shared scheduling system as a set of backend microservices.
  • Built the payment-retry services for three subscription products, including failure tracking and automatic cancellation after the final retry.
  • Integrated the full Stripe lifecycle: Payment Intents, subscriptions, webhooks, refunds, fees and automatic card updates.

Who I worked with

  • Product and Design, to turn requirements into schedule rules and customer flows.
  • Other engineers on the team, through design reviews, pairing and code review.

05Key decisions and rejected alternatives

Chosen

One shared scheduling engine used by every product, with product-specific rules as configuration.

Rejected

Separate scheduling logic in each product. Why it was rejected (duplicated bugs, inconsistent behaviour, cost to change?).

Chosen

Idempotent payment creation, keyed on schedule and due date, so repeating a run or a request cannot create a second charge.

Rejected

Relying on the run executing exactly once. Confirm this matches how duplicates were prevented in practice.

Chosen

Capped, automatic retries with cancellation after the final attempt, so failed schedules don't retry forever.

Rejected

Manual follow-up of every failed payment. The retry spacing and cap you used, if you're happy to share them.

06Implementation highlights

  • Frequencies: daily, weekly, monthly and seasonal schedules from a single model.
  • Lifecycle changes: pauses, cancellations and payment-method changes handled without breaking the next run.
  • Card updates: Stripe's automatic card updates removed manual payment-method changes for customers.
  • Retries: failure tracking per payment and automatic cancellation after the final retry.

07Testing, observability, security and reliability

Testing

Unit tests with mocks for payment calculations; integration and API tests in CI; Playwright end-to-end tests for critical customer journeys.

Reliability

Idempotent payment creation, de-duplicated webhook handling, state that only moves forward, and capped retries.

Observability

Self-serve reporting on payment runs through a DynamoDB-to-Athena pipeline with Metabase dashboards.

Alerting and logging you used (for example CloudWatch alarms on failed runs).

Security

Card details stay with Stripe; services work with Stripe identifiers.
